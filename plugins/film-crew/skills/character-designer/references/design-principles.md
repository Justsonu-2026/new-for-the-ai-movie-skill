## 1. Shape language
A common convention in animation and illustration design:
- **Circles / soft curves:** friendly, warm, approachable, youthful.
- **Squares / blocks:** stable, strong, stubborn, dependable.
- **Triangles / sharp angles:** dynamic, dangerous, cunning, unstable.
Mix them deliberately: a kind giant (square body, round face); a nervous genius (triangle hair, round
glasses). In photoreal work, apply it through build, haircut, posture and garment cut.

## 2. Silhouette
Fill the full-body figure solid black. A good design is identifiable from the outline alone: distinctive
hair mass, garment shape (hood, long coat, wide trousers), posture, a carried object. Test at the size the
character appears in a wide shot. Silhouettes of two characters in the same scene must differ.

## 3. Color anchors
Give each character one or two signature colors that appear on them in every look (a yellow raincoat, a
red scarf) and that no other character uses. Anchors keep identity readable when faces are small or the
model drifts. Pick anchors that sit well inside the style bible's palette and contrast with the backgrounds
of their main locations. Record each anchor with a hex value and its name.

## 4. Proportions and height
- State **height in cm** and **head-heights** (realistic adult ≈ 7-8 heads; children ≈ 4-6; stylized
  characters vary). Keep the number fixed.
- State build (slight, wiry, athletic, stocky, heavy), shoulder width relative to hips, leg-to-torso ratio
  if stylized.
- Lineup heights are relative facts the cinematographer and director rely on for eyelines and framing.

## 5. Detail budget
Every detail must survive regeneration. Prefer a few large, distinctive features over many small ones.
High-risk details for AI generation: small text or logos, complex repeating patterns, asymmetric jewelry,
many buttons, intricate tattoos, glasses with thin frames in motion. Keep them only if the story needs them,
and then describe them precisely (side, size, shape, color).

## 6. Design variants
- **Photoreal human:** describe as for casting: age range, face structure, skin tone and texture, hair,
  build; avoid celebrity comparisons (likeness risk); avoid "beautiful/handsome" — describe features.
- **Stylized / animated:** specify construction (head shape, eye size relative to head, line weight if
  2D, material if 3D), proportions in heads, simplification rules (e.g. four fingers), and the expression
  system (eyebrow shapes, mouth shapes). The animation-director uses these for poses and lip-sync.
- **Creature / robot / non-human:** specify the anatomy logic (how it moves, joints, number of limbs),
  materials and surface (rust, fur length, scales), where its "face" reads emotion, scale against a human,
  and sound-making parts (feeds sound-designer).

## 7. Costume design notes
- Costume tells story: class, job, era, climate, personality, arc (a costume can change as the character does).
- Design for the action: running needs footwear that can run; fights need sleeves that move.
- Layers: outer, mid, inner, footwear, accessories; specify which layers are visible in which looks.
- Materials affect light: wet leather shines, wool absorbs, silk reflects, sequins sparkle. Name them.
- Condition states: wet, dirty, torn, bloodied, singed — define where on the garment and how much.
- Era and place: check the costume fits the world rules in story.md.
