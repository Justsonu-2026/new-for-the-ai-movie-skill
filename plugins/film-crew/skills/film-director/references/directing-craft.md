## 1. Scene analysis
Answer per scene, one line each:
- **Literal event:** what happens on the surface.
- **Objectives:** what each character wants from the other(s) in this scene.
- **Obstacle:** what stands in the way (the other character, circumstances, themselves).
- **Tactics:** how they try (charm, threaten, deflect, plead) — tactics change when they fail.
- **Subtext:** what the scene is really about.
- **The turn:** the moment the scene's value flips (from the scene card), and who causes it.
- **Audience position:** do we know more than the characters (suspense), less (mystery), or the same?

## 2. Visual thesis
One photographable idea that organizes the scene's choices, stated as a change you can see:
- "The room's door stays in every shot behind her until she finally walks through it."
- "We start wide and loose; every beat Owen lies, the frame tightens on Maya."
If it cannot be shot, it is a theme, not a thesis. Point to the shots that carry it.

## 3. Beats
A beat is a change in the balance of pressure: new information, a new tactic, a decision, a reveal. Shots
serve beats; one beat can be one shot or five. Mark the scene's biggest beat (usually the turn) — it gets
the strongest image and the most time.

## 4. Playable actions
Direct with transitive verbs that one character does to another: to warn, to test, to seduce, to belittle,
to reassure, to stall, to accuse. Each produces behavior: where they look, how close they stand, what their
hands do, how fast they speak. Write the behavior, because it is what the camera and the video model see:
- Result direction (avoid): "she is angry".
- Playable direction: "to make him leave — she keeps her back to him, folds the shirt too hard, answers
  without looking."

## 5. Blocking patterns
- **Motivated moves:** a character moves because of an intention (to get closer, to escape, to fetch, to
  hide) — never just to vary the picture.
- **Triangles:** three characters arranged in depth read better than three in a line.
- **Business:** give characters tasks with props (cooking, packing, repairing); tasks reveal character and
  give the camera action during dialogue.
- **Entrances and exits:** decide the side and keep screen direction consistent with the previous and
  next scene (left-to-right as "forward" in a journey, for example).
- **Depth staging:** foreground/background separation lets one shot carry two beats (one character in
  focus listening, the other behind).
- **Use the set:** doors, windows, stairs and landmarks are blocking tools.

## 6. Power, distance and height
- Higher in frame / standing over / center of frame → power; lower / edge of frame → weakness.
- Approaching → pressure or intimacy; backing away → retreat; turning the back → refusal or vulnerability.
- Barriers between characters (tables, doorframes, railings) express separation; removing them is a beat.
- Eyelines: who looks at whom, and who avoids it, tells the relationship.

## 7. Reaction and stillness
The listener often matters more than the speaker. Plan reaction shots at the beats that change the
listener. Stillness after movement is a strong beat; hold it long enough to land (often 1-3 s on screen).
