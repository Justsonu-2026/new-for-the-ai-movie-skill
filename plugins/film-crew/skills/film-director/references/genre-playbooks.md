# Genre Playbooks

Apply the dominant genre of the scene. Each playbook: what the audience must feel, then the directing
levers. Action/fight scenes go to the action-director; animated work to the animation-director.

## Contents
1. Drama
2. Comedy
3. Horror
4. Romance
5. Thriller and suspense
6. Mixing genres

## 1. Drama
- **Feel:** the character's inner change, felt through small behavior.
- **Levers:** restraint; let performances play in MS/MCU with time to breathe; reactions over speeches;
  motivated, slow moves (push-ins at realizations); natural motivated light; hold on silence after a turn.
- **Avoid:** over-coverage that cuts away from the key emotion; music doing the acting.

## 2. Comedy
- **Feel:** surprise and release; the audience is ahead of or exactly with the joke.
- **Levers:**
  - **Frame the joke whole:** show setup and payoff in the same frame when possible (wider sizes, FS/WS),
    so the audience sees the cause and the effect at once.
  - **Timing:** the pause before the payoff; the reaction after it — cut to the reaction at the laugh, not before.
  - **Rule of three:** setup, reinforce, subvert.
  - **Static camera** lets physical comedy read; movement can kill the timing.
  - **High-key light**, brighter palette unless the comedy is dark.
  - **Reaction shots** of the straight character carry many jokes.
- **Avoid:** cutting mid-gag; close-ups that hide the physical cause.

## 3. Horror
- **Feel:** dread (anticipation) more than shock; the audience fears what they cannot see.
- **Levers:**
  - **Negative space:** leave empty areas (dark doorways, the edge of the frame) where something could appear.
  - **Withhold:** delay the reveal; show the reaction before the threat; partial views (hands, shadows).
  - **Slow pace, then break:** long holds and slow creeping moves; a sudden cut or sound for the scare.
  - **Low-key light**, hard shadows, single sources, darkness that hides the frame edges.
  - **Sound** does half the work: plan silence before scares (tell the sound-designer).
  - **Point of view:** shots from an unknown watcher's position make the audience complicit.
- **Avoid:** showing the monster fully too early; jump scares without build-up; constant shaky camera.

## 4. Romance
- **Feel:** attraction, longing, the distance between two people closing (or not).
- **Levers:**
  - **Distance as the story:** start characters separated in frame (separate singles, barriers between),
    bring them together into two-shots as they connect.
  - **Eyelines and looks:** the stolen glance, the held look; cut on looks.
  - **Longer lenses, shallow focus** isolate the couple from the world; soft, warm, flattering light.
  - **Slow movement:** arcs and slow push-ins; time slows at the moment of connection.
  - **Hands and small touches** as inserts (keep short for AI).
- **Avoid:** cutting away at the moment of contact; over-scored moments.

## 5. Thriller and suspense
- **Feel:** tension from information the audience has (Hitchcock's bomb-under-the-table principle: show
  the audience the bomb, then make them wait).
- **Levers:** show the threat to the audience early; cross-cut between the threat and the unaware
  character; tighten sizes and shorten shots as the clock runs down; use inserts of the ticking element;
  cool palette with hot accents; keep geography clear so the audience can measure danger.
- **Avoid:** confusing geography; revealing everything verbally.

## 6. Mixing genres
Pick the dominant genre per scene and borrow levers deliberately: a horror-comedy frames the joke wide but
lights it low-key; a romantic thriller uses romance distance rules inside a suspense structure. State the
mix in the scene analysis.
