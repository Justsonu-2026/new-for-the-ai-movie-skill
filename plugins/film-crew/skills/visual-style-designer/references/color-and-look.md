## 1. Building the master palette
- Start from the story's emotional poles (safety vs danger, past vs present, inside vs outside) and give
  each pole a hue family.
- Assign roles: **dominant** (most of the frame, usually environment), **secondary**, **accent** (small
  amounts, draws the eye), **danger/alarm**, **warmth/safety**, **reserved anchors** (character colors no
  set may use, so characters pop).
- Use contrast of hue (complementary accents), value (light against dark) and saturation (one saturated
  element in a muted frame is the strongest pointer to where to look).
- Name colors concretely ("sodium-vapor orange", "wet-asphalt black") with hex values.

## 2. The color script
A color script maps color and light across the whole film so the look carries the emotional arc. Pixar's
color scripts are the best-known example: production designer Lou Romano described the one for *The
Incredibles* as giving "mostly the emotional tone of each scene".

| Scene | Beat | Dominant | Accent | Warm/cool | Saturation | Key | Change vs previous |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SC01 | routine, lonely | blue-grey | sodium orange | cool | low | low-key | — |
| SC02 | chase, danger | wet black | magenta neon | cold with hot accents | high accents | very low-key | more contrast, hotter accent |
| SC03 | revelation | dim amber | label white | warmer | low | low-key, soft | warmth enters |

Rules: save the most extreme palette for the climax; make the final scene answer the first (mirror or
reversal); keep 3-5 anchors per scene.

## 3. Contrast and key
- **High-key:** bright, low contrast, soft shadows — comedy, innocence, safety.
- **Low-key:** dark, strong contrast, hard shadows — thriller, horror, noir, intimacy at night.
- **Motivated light:** every light in the frame has a believable source in the set; production-designer
  lists the sources, the cinematographer uses them.
- State the contrast ratio feel in words ("shadows fall to near black", "shadows keep detail").

## 4. Lens character
Look-level choices only (focal lengths per shot belong to the cinematographer):
- spherical (clean, natural) vs anamorphic look (oval bokeh, horizontal flares, wide aspect);
- depth-of-field tendency (shallow isolation vs deep staging);
- distortion tolerance (wide-angle intimacy vs compressed long-lens look);
- flare and halation policy.

## 5. Texture
Grain (none / fine / heavy), halation around highlights, sharpness, bloom, vignetting, gate weave, skin
rendering (natural pores vs smoothed), for 2D: line weight and color, shadow style (flat cel, soft
gradient), paper or brush texture; for 3D: material feel (clay, plastic, painterly), subsurface softness.

## 6. Medium vocabulary
Be exact: "photoreal digital cinema with 35 mm film emulation", "16 mm documentary handheld look",
"2D cel animation, bold uniform outline, two-tone shading", "watercolor storybook illustration",
"stylized 3D animation with soft rounded forms and subsurface skin", "stop-motion felt and clay with
visible fingerprints", "anime-style 2D with painted backgrounds".

## 7. Writing the style lock
30-70 words, ordered: medium → rendering/texture → light and contrast → palette anchors → lens character.
Example:
> Photoreal digital cinema with fine 35 mm film grain and soft halation on highlights, low-key motivated
> lighting with deep shadows that fall to near black, wet reflective surfaces, palette of wet-asphalt
> black, sodium-vapor orange and magenta neon accents, spherical lens look with shallow depth of field and
> no lens flares.
No story words, no emotions, no brand or artist names, no camera moves.
