## 1. Thinking characters
Audiences believe an animated character is alive when they can see it think. Show the sequence:
**perceive → think → decide → act → react to the result.** The eyes move first, the head follows, the
body last. Hesitations, double-takes and small corrections are thought made visible.

## 2. One clear idea per pose
Each key pose expresses one idea (defiant, shrinking, reaching). If a pose tries to show two, split it into
two poses over time. Avoid "twinning" (both arms or legs doing the same thing symmetrically) unless the
character is stiff by design.

## 3. Gesture and contrast
- Build poses on a **line of action** (a single curve through the spine): C-curves and S-curves read as
  alive; straight lines read as stiff or rigid characters.
- Contrast between consecutive poses makes the change readable: closed → open, low → high, compressed → stretched.
- Specific gestures beat generic ones: a character who rubs the strap of her bag when nervous, not
  "nervous hands".

## 4. Staging for clarity
- Pose so the action reads in silhouette (arms away from the body when they matter).
- Present the important part (hand with the key, the face) toward camera.
- One main action at a time per shot; secondary characters hold or make small moves.

## 5. Eyes and brows
- Eye direction tells what the character attends to; change it before head turns.
- Blinks mark thought changes; time blinks at decisions, not at random.
- Brows carry most of the emotion in stylized faces; use the model sheet's brow shapes.

## 6. Listening and reacting
Listeners are not frozen: small breathing, eye darts, a reaction at the key word. Plan the reaction pose
at the beat where the speaker's line lands.
