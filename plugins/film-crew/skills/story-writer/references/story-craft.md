## 1. Runtime scaling

Screenplay convention: about one page per minute of screen time. The counts below are production
heuristics for AI-generated films (each recurring character, location and set piece multiplies reference
images, keyframes and failure risk), not industry rules. Exceed them only with a reason.

| Runtime | Main characters | Locations | Scenes | Story shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15-30 s | 1 | 1 | 1 | one situation, one turn (a joke, reveal or image) |
| 30-90 s | 1-2 | 1-2 | 2-4 | setup → complication → turn/twist → resolution |
| 90 s-3 min | 2-3 | 2-3 | 3-6 | five-beat short (below) |
| 3-10 min | 2-4 | 3-5 | 6-15 | compressed three acts, one subplot at most |
| 10-30 min | 3-6 | 4-8 | 12-35 | three acts, A and B story |
| feature | as needed | as needed | 40-70 | full structure (Save the Cat, sequences) |

## 2. Choosing a structure

| Structure | Parts | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Five-beat short | 1 Setup (want + world) · 2 Inciting incident · 3 Escalation (attempts fail, cost rises) · 4 Climax: decisive choice or twist · 5 Resolution image | most shorts under 3 min |
| Three acts | Setup · Confrontation · Resolution, with turning points near 25%, 50% (midpoint reversal), 75% (low point → final push) | anything with a clear goal, 3 min and up |
| Story Circle (Dan Harmon) | You · Need · Go · Search · Find · Take · Return · Change | character-change stories, episodic TV |
| Kishōtenketsu | Introduction · Development · Twist (unexpected new element) · Conclusion (reconciles) | gentle, contemplative or comic stories with no antagonist |
| Save the Cat (Blake Snyder) | Opening Image, Theme Stated, Set-Up, Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two, B Story, Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break into Three, Finale, Final Image | features and long-form commercial genre stories |
| Loop / ring | ends where it began, changed | dreamlike, music video, poetic shorts |

Robert McKee's test applies at every level: a scene, sequence or act must turn a value in the character's
life (safe → in danger, trusted → betrayed). If nothing turns, it is not a story unit.

## 3. Character questions

Answer for every major character; keep each answer to one line.
- **Want:** the concrete external goal the audience can see them pursue.
- **Need:** what they must learn or become; usually opposed to the want.
- **Flaw / wound:** the behavior (and its cause) that blocks the need.
- **The lie they believe:** the false belief the story will test.
- **Contradiction:** one surprising combination (a gentle bouncer, a cowardly hero's fearless kid sister).
- **Risk:** what they lose if they fail, personally.
- **Visible trait:** one habit, object or physical choice the camera can show (feeds design and acting).
- **Voice sketch:** how they talk in five words (feeds screenwriter and voice-director).
- **Arc:** from → to, in one line. Flat-arc characters (who change the world, not themselves) are valid: say so.

Opposing force: give it a want and a reason it believes it is right. Nature, systems and time count as
opposing forces; give them escalating pressure.

## 4. Causality and the beat outline

Write beats as cause and effect. Between beats, the honest connector must be "therefore" (consequence)
or "but" (complication) — the heuristic popularised by South Park creators Trey Parker and Matt Stone.
"And then" means the beats are a list, not a story: merge, cut or add the missing cause.

Beat format:
`B3 | Maya climbs onto the roof to avoid the courier gang | BUT the roof door locks behind her | value: in
control → trapped | cost: she drops the scanner | est. 15 s`

## 5. Twists that work

A good twist is surprising, fair and consequential.
- **Fair:** plant at least two clues that are visible on screen before the reveal (an object, a line with a
  double meaning, a reaction that seems odd). Clues should read one way on first viewing and another way
  after the reveal.
- **Recontextualizing:** the reveal changes the meaning of earlier beats, not just the next one.
- **Consequential:** the protagonist must act on it; a twist in the last second with no response is a gag.

Types: identity (who someone is), motive (why they did it), perspective (whose story it is), time (when it
happens), situation (where/what this really is), the protagonist's own role (they caused it).
Avoid: twists that invalidate everything (it was all a dream/simulation) unless the story is about that.

Film-friendly clues are visual: an address on a label, a matching scar, a photo, a recurring sound.

## 6. Theme

State theme as an argument, not a topic: not "loneliness" but "you cannot be helped until you let someone
see you need help". The climax should test the argument through the protagonist's choice. Never have a
character state the theme outright at the end; let the final image carry it.

## 7. Emotional journey map

For each beat, note the audience's intended feeling and the protagonist's state. Look for:
- a clear starting emotion and a different ending emotion;
- at least one moment of relief or humor in tense stories, and one moment of threat in gentle ones;
- the biggest emotional moment landing at the climax, not before it.

## 8. Cliché watch-list

Avoid, or subvert deliberately: waking up to an alarm clock as the opening; "it was all a dream"; amnesia
as the mystery; the chosen one; a mentor dying to motivate the hero; villain explains the plan; rescue by
coincidence (deus ex machina); the phone with no signal; a character looking at a photo to show grief;
running in slow motion toward each other; the stranger who turns out to be the protagonist's parent.

## 9. Writing for AI production

These make a story easier to produce well with current image and video models:
- Show story points through faces, bodies and clear objects; avoid points that depend on readable text
  (text renders unreliably; if text matters, flag it for VFX).
- Prefer a few strong locations over many brief ones; each location needs reference plates.
- Limit simultaneous physical interaction between several characters (grappling, dancing, crowds).
- Fine hand actions (tying, writing, picking locks) are high risk; keep them short or show the result.
- Keep costume changes rare and motivated; each look needs its own reference sheet.
- Give each character a distinct silhouette or color anchor so they stay distinguishable.
