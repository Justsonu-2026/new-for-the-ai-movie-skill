## 1. Motivated lighting
Every light should appear to come from a source in the world: sun, sky, window, lamp, screen, fire, sign.
The production-designer lists sources per location and time; you decide how each lights the subject.
Stylized unmotivated light (pure color washes, theatrical spot) only if the style bible allows it.

## 2. Key, fill, rim and alternatives
- **Key:** main light; its side and height define the face's shape and mood.
- **Fill:** lifts shadows; less fill = more contrast. "No fill" is a valid, dramatic choice.
- **Rim/back light:** separates the subject from the background; essential in dark scenes.
- **Practicals:** visible lamps/signs in frame; often motivate the key.
- **Bounce/ambient:** soft overall level from walls, sky.
- **Negative fill:** blocking light to deepen shadows on one side.
Common setups: **short side key** (key on the side of the face turned away from camera — sculpted, moody),
**broad key** (key on the side toward camera — open, flatter), **top light** (harsh, isolating),
**under light** (unnatural, horror), **silhouette** (backlight only).

## 3. Quality: hard and soft
Hard light (small or distant source: sun, bare bulb, neon tube at distance) → crisp shadows, texture,
tension. Soft light (large or diffused source: overcast sky, big window, bounced light) → gentle shadow
edges, calm, beauty.

## 4. Color temperature and color
Approximate values: candle ~1900 K, tungsten bulb ~3200 K, sunrise/sunset ~3000-3500 K, daylight ~5600 K,
overcast/shade ~6500-7500 K, blue hour higher still. Lower K looks warmer (orange), higher K cooler (blue).
Colored sources (neon, signs, screens) are named by color. Mixed color temperature between key and
background creates separation (warm interior vs blue window).

## 5. Contrast ratio and key
Describe contrast as a ratio feel: low (soft, high-key, shadows keep detail), medium, high (shadows to
near black, low-key). Keep it consistent with the style bible's contrast and the color script row.

## 6. Day, night, interiors, exteriors
- **Day exterior:** the sun's direction relative to the floor plan; backlit faces with bounce look
  cinematic; overcast for soft.
- **Night exterior:** motivated by streetlights, signs, windows, moon; always give an edge/rim so shapes read;
  wet surfaces add reflections.
- **Day interior:** windows as key; the inside darker than outside unless filled.
- **Night interior:** practicals as key; pools of light and darkness; the window becomes a cool fill or rim.

## 7. Lighting diagram format
```
SETUP A (SC03, landing, night) — camera on the south (stairs) side
            N
   [painted 3 wall]
 W [skylight: rain, cool moonlight top]            E
   Maya ●→ faces E           [D2 door: warm 2700 K spill through chain gap]
   key: caged bulb above-left of Maya (W wall), hard, 3200 K
   rim: skylight top-back, cool
   fill: none (shadow side near black)
   camera: S, 1.5 m height
```

## 8. Consistency across shots
- Within a setup, the key stays on the same side relative to the set; when you shoot the reverse, the
  light falls on the other side of the face — that is correct and must be stated.
- Light doesn't change level or color between shots of the same moment unless motivated (a door opens).
- Track time of day across scenes in a sequence (dusk can't jump to midday).

## 9. Writing light for generators
Describe sources, directions and colors in plain words, plus 3-5 palette anchors (Sora 2 guide: name the
sources and tones, e.g. "soft window light with warm lamp fill, cool rim from hallway"):
"hard magenta neon key from frame right, cool blue rim from behind on frame left, no fill, deep shadows,
wet surfaces reflecting neon". Avoid "dramatic lighting" or "cinematic lighting" without specifics.
