## 1. The three jobs of a line
A line must do at least one: **reveal character** (how they see the world), **advance conflict** (someone
wants something from someone), **carry subtext** (it means more than it says). A line that only conveys
information the image already shows is cut.

## 2. Distinct voices
Build each speaking character a voice profile from story.md's five-word voice sketch:
- vocabulary (plain / technical / slangy / formal), sentence length, rhythm;
- what they never say directly; what they say when scared; a verbal habit (used sparingly);
- how they address others (names, nicknames, titles, nothing).
Test: cover the character cues on a page; you should be able to tell who speaks. If two characters sound
alike, change one's sentence length and directness first.

Avoid phonetic spelling of accents and heavy dialect; suggest it once in the character intro and through
word choice. The voice-director handles accent in performance.

## 3. Subtext
People rarely say what they want. Let characters talk *around* the real subject: a fight about the dishes
that is about leaving; "Did you eat?" meaning "I'm sorry". Write the surface line, and know the subtext (note it in the scene
card, not on the page). Strong subtext needs the audience to know the real stakes from earlier scenes.

## 4. Exposition
- Put information inside conflict: the character who needs to know must fight to get it.
- Withhold: give the audience the minimum to follow, and make them lean in for the rest.
- Never "As you know, Bob": characters don't tell each other what both already know.
- Prefer showing: an eviction notice taped to the door beats a speech about rent.
- One fact per exchange; spread facts across scenes.

## 5. Rhythm and timing for AI production
- Speech rate: about 2.5 words per second for normal conversation (≈150 words per minute); slower for
  emotional or careful speech, faster for arguments.
- A video generation clip holds only a few seconds of dialogue. Sora 2's guide notes a 4 s shot holds one
  or two short exchanges. Keep most lines under ~12 words; split longer speeches across shots or use
  voice-over over images.
- Lip-sync is more reliable on shorter lines with the speaker facing camera; long lines spoken off-screen
  (O.S.) or in V.O. avoid lip-sync risk.
- Mark overlapping speech only when it matters; it is hard to generate and to mix.

## 6. Silence and reactions
The most powerful moment in a scene is often a character *not* answering. Write the reaction as action:
`Owen opens his mouth. Closes it. Hands her the key.` Give listeners actions; the director needs
something to shoot during long lines.

## 7. Line-level fixes
| Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|
| On-the-nose ("I'm angry because you left") | Say it sideways; replace with behavior or a deflection |
| Speech longer than 3 lines | Break with an interruption, an action, or cut to the essential clause |
| Every line is a complete sentence | Use fragments, interruptions, questions answered with questions |
| Characters use each other's names a lot | Cut names except for emphasis |
| Jokes explained | Cut the explanation; trust the reaction |
| Same idea said twice in a scene | Keep the stronger version |
| Ending a scene on a line that summarizes | End on an action or image instead |
